Pricing Your First Product: Simple Rules That Beat Guesswork

By the 222248 editorial team · Updated 2026-10-26 · 7 min read

Your first price is a hypothesis, not a commitment. But a bad hypothesis wastes your launch: too low and you attract the wrong buyers and cannot fund growth; too high without proof and nobody tries you. Use three anchors.

Anchor 1: The value to the buyer

Estimate what the problem costs the customer each month in money or time. If your product saves a freelancer five hours a month and their time is worth a known hourly rate, the value is easy to state. Pricing at a fraction of that value is an easy yes.

Anchor 2: The alternatives

List what buyers use today: competing tools, an assistant, a spreadsheet, doing nothing. Your price should make sense next to those options. If you are cheaper, explain why that is not a quality signal; if you are more expensive, show the extra outcome.

Anchor 3: Your break-even

Know how many sales cover your fixed costs. Use our break-even calculator: enter monthly fixed costs, your price and your cost per sale. If you need thousands of customers to cover a modest bill, the price — or the model — is wrong.

Rules of thumb

  • One offer, one price for launch. Add tiers once you know who buys.
  • Round, confident numbers for services; charm prices for low-ticket products.
  • Charge from day one. Free users give feedback about free products.
  • Raise prices for new customers as proof accumulates; grandfather early buyers.
  • Offer a guarantee instead of a discount to reduce risk.

If nobody objects to your price, it is probably too low. A few “that's expensive” replies alongside real purchases is a healthy sign.
