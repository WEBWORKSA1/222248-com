How to Validate a Business Idea in 8 Hours (Before You Build Anything)

By the 222248 editorial team · Updated 2026-10-26 · 8 min read

Validation is not asking friends whether they like your idea. It is collecting evidence that strangers have a problem, are already spending to solve it, and will switch to you. You can collect a surprising amount of that evidence in one focused working day.

Step 1 — Write the problem, not the product (30 minutes)

Describe the problem in the customer's words. “Freelance designers lose hours chasing unpaid invoices” is testable. “An AI invoicing platform” is not — it is a solution looking for a problem. Name the person, the situation and the cost.

Step 2 — Find where the problem is discussed (90 minutes)

Search forums, community threads, product reviews and Q&A sites for people describing the pain. Three-star reviews of existing tools are gold: they tell you what buyers already pay for and what still annoys them. Save the exact phrases people use; they become your headline later.

Step 3 — Five problem interviews (3 hours)

Book five short calls or chats with people who match your target. Ask about the past, not the future:

  1. When did this last happen to you?
  2. What did you do about it?
  3. What did that cost — in money, time or stress?
  4. What have you tried that did not work?
  5. If this disappeared tomorrow, what would change for you?

Never pitch during these calls. Your job is to listen for workarounds. A spreadsheet, a virtual assistant or a paid tool they dislike all prove the problem is real.

Step 4 — Smoke-test page (2 hours)

Publish a one-page site with the promise, three benefits, a price, and a button such as “Reserve early access.” The button leads to a short form or a refundable deposit. This measures intent, which is far stronger than opinion.

Step 5 — Send targeted traffic (1 hour)

Share the page with your interviewees and in two or three places where your buyers gather. Be transparent that it is early. Small, targeted traffic beats large, random traffic for validation.

The go / no-go rule

Proceed when at least 3 of 5 interviewees describe a paid or time-costly workaround and your smoke test produces real sign-ups or deposits from people you do not know. Otherwise, change one variable — the audience, the problem or the price — and test again.

Common mistakes

  • Counting compliments as demand.
  • Interviewing people who would never buy (friends, other founders).
  • Testing ten ideas at once with no clear audience for any.
  • Skipping a price on the smoke test — free sign-ups tell you little about willingness to pay.
